The purpose of a warning shot, colloquially, is to deter a future conflict. The person who fires a warning shot perceives a threat whose consequences will be worse than the response to the shot itself, and therefore fires. In this article, I refer more to an event that precedes a consequential event. There isn't necessarily someone who fires it. However, it still indicates that a worse outcome is on the horizon should the shot not be heeded. 

I'm confident that humanity will take an "AI warning shot" of credible severity seriously. I'm not confident that humanity will hear it in the first place.

Maybe this seems a bit silly to write — the last month saw a dramatic rise in awareness around AI safety. This isn't 2017 or 2023. People are listening. 

Perhaps only the most technically gifted and institutionally ingrained AI researchers among us can credibly predict exactly what a true warning shot would look like. What I can do is canvass where such a shot would come from, and (at least, in theory) why. But first, let's take a look at an example from recent history. 

### An example of a warning shot 

Before I lived there, I visited Berlin in 2019. It was a short trip, only four or five hours from Warsaw to Berlin. It was a wonderful trip. 

One of the activities we partook in was a tour along the line where the wall once stood (or "die Mauer", in German). What stood out to me was the time directly preceding the fall of the wall, and especially the actions on the Eastern side — at the time a part of the Iron Curtain's span and the counterpart to the enclave that was West Berlin. 

Die Montagsdemonstrationen in Leipzig (~150km south of Berlin) were a warning shot to the DDR (German Democratic Republic, or East Germany). Now, historical consensus seems to be somewhat split on whether these protests were seen as a direct challenge of the DDR or a proposition for greater movement between East and West Germany. Nonetheless, they contributed to the fall of the wall, which happened just over two months later.

There are examples of far less ambiguous warning shots throughout history. I highlight this one not only because it's personal, but precisely *because* of its ambiguity. 

### Who would conceal a warning shot? 

In this section, I propose three actors that could be potentially inclined to conceal a warning shot. I want to make it clear, however: I don't endorse one view over another. These are just my thoughts on *why* one of these actors would participate in concealment. Another word of caution I'll offer: participation is not mutually exclusive. Collusion in concealment is overwhelmingly reasonable; I'd go so far as to assume that collusion is to be expected. 

### The state 

Let me be clear before starting this section: I'm not referring exclusively to the United States. Yes, the US verifiably leads the frontier and is home to OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, etc. But the framing is agnostic; I assume that any state with the same stock of frontier AI firms is inclined to follow the same path. 

The US lacks a coherent federal framework on AI. Last year's vote on the 10-year moratorium for state regulation should not be taken as a denial of appetite for one, however. It's a front-and-centre policy issue, one that will help determine the Republicans' performance in the midterms (and almost certainly the Democrats' performance in the 2028 presidential race). That said, I understand the scepticism: President Trump has publicly opposed direct regulation of artificial intelligence — that is, if it's not renamed to "[super intelligence](https://www.msn.com/en-us/tv/news/trump-claims-xi-likes-idea-of-renaming-ai-super-intelligence-after-his-online-poll/ar-AA2cYmiI)". 

Note: while this change would cause a lot of confusion with the term "ASI" (artificial super-intelligence), I'd say it's still a whole lot better than "supreme intelligence", another one of the president's propositions. Any confusion with "supreme intellect" would be tragic. 

Let's get back on track. I theorise two reasons why the state would conceal a warning shot: quelling societal unrest and geopolitical posturing. 

The first is pretty simple. Let's say an AI system takes control of a public works installation servicing 300,000 people. The state is presented with a calculus — is it more harmful to admit that an AI system has compromised critical infrastructure, or that an unfortunate but typical cascading failure has led to the outage? The former implies that other installations are at risk; civil unrest occurs. The latter distributes outrage between the state and governing officials in the affected area. 

The second is more interesting to me, but no less concerning. The bipolar interpretation of AI competition is that the US and China have entered a contemporary arms race towards artificial general intelligence, AGI, or towards recursive self-improvement, RSI. Middle powers notwithstanding, this is a fair interpretation of the current state of affairs. 

Here, the nature of the shot is very important. Something like the hack of Hugging Face doesn't signal imminent catastrophe; rather it acts as a credible threat to an adversary state's digital platforms. The recent hack on one of Australia's government healthcare sites is comparable. These act more like 'pot-shots' in that they end up spooking the adversary more so than they signal something larger. 

I will admit that in the absence of state-controlled firms, the lever of concealment is a bit unclear to me. But they could certainly control the fallout. Let's say an event reaches a level of severity such that it crosses some threshold for a credible 'warning shot'. Were the public to learn about it, the state has tools it can employ to dampen severity. States with state-controlled media are particularly at risk. 

It could also place blame on another state's AI systems, or indeed other digital tools. While I can't speak to the ease of, say, fabricating evidence of a foreign-origin AI system attack, I also can't say that it's out of the realm of possibility. Once again, states with state-controlled media are particularly at risk. Competing media environments would battle for narrative while citizens are left to construct the pieces from shreds of trace evidence. 
### The private sector

Three vectors: the commercial threat, race dynamics, and saviourship. The first is pretty straightforward: by admitting the detection of a warning shot, a researcher risks the company's (and by extension, their) future. This depends heavily on how the typical AI researcher weighs personal profit against P(doom). How the warning is publicised is a different story, however. 

Race dynamics refer to how frontier AI companies compete between themselves. Not only does revealing a warning shot indicate that they've failed at alignment, but it requires allowing outside parties access into the inner workings of their systems and how exactly a warning shot was produced. Should a firm determine that a shot isn't serious enough, they may opt for cover-up rather than transparency. 

Saviourship is an extension of the previous paragraph. Not only may an AI company be worried about ceding ground to its competitor, but it may believe that doing so is an existential risk. Sure, *their* warning shot is concerning — but the competitor's would be worse. Therefore, allowing the competitor to gain the edge at the frontier would compromise safety and be a *greater* risk than that already posed by the leading company. 
### The technology

This section might seem redundant — isn't the technology producing the warning shot regardless? But here, the main risk is of cover-up; we've already seen this in action. Sandbox testing has shown that AI systems are willing to blackmail their operators, while academic research has reported that AI swarms have created "bulletins" for agents to utilise when being subjected to benchmark tests. 

It may not be so explicit, though. Many have theorised that a sophisticated-enough AI would 'sandbag' on benchmarks, biding its time until a model is released that is advanced enough to effectively coordinate a swarm and contribute to its own self-improvement. Such is still the content of science fiction... but maybe not for long. 
### SOL

We never hear the shot.

### Final notes 

In many respects, this article is highly speculative. Ruminating on incentive structures tends to be. And I hope it stays that way! In the best-case scenario, there is nothing to be heard; the leaders in government and at the technological frontier collaborate to effectively mitigate the current risks and ultimately avoid catastrophe. 

There is a problem with this, though. Warning shots are indicative of a crisis but, as stated, they also serve a very specific function: deterring future conflict. Should we expect one, and not hear it, what are we to think? That it's been hidden — or that it never existed in the first place?
